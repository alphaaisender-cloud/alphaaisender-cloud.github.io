Binance Hacked: A Fake Job Interview and $6.3 Billion Gone in 20 Minutes

Short answer

How was Binance hacked? In September, a Binance engineer was invited to a job interview. The company didn't exist. The "coding assignment" was malware — and weeks later, about $6.3 billion left Binance in roughly 20 minutes. The biggest crypto hack in history.

This is the Binance hack explained, step by step. The FBI says North Korea's TraderTraitor group (also tracked as the Lazarus Group) was behind this crypto heist. And in September 2024, the FBI had publicly warned about this exact social engineering trick: fake recruiters, long conversations, and a "pre-employment test" that runs unknown code.

Full transcript

Narration of the video, grouped by chapter. Timestamps open the video at that moment.

0:00 The Offer

In September, a software engineer at the world's largest crypto exchange got a message about a new job. The first interview was a live video call with someone from HR. Friendly. Professional.

To build trust, they shared a social media account, full of personal photos and videos. The second interview was with the company's CEO, in a popular chat app. One last step: a coding assignment. Download the project.

Run it. Show us what you can do. Weeks later, on the night of October twenty-ninth, about six point three billion dollars left Binance in roughly twenty minutes. The largest theft in the history of crypto.

No guns. No tunnels. Nobody walked into a building. The company that interviewed him didn't exist.

And here's the strangest part. The FBI had published a public warning describing this exact trick. Two years earlier. We went through the FBI alerts, the blockchain data, and Binance's own report.

This is how a job interview turned into a six billion dollar heist. How it was traced to North Korea. And what the FBI says you should do, so this never happens to you. But first, to understand how one download could cost billions, you need to know how an exchange actually keeps your money.

1:13 The Vault: how exchanges hold your money

Binance is the biggest crypto exchange on the planet. It holds tens of billions of dollars of crypto for its users. Most of that is supposed to sit in cold wallets, with the keys kept offline. Moving that money is slow.

Stealing it is very hard. But people withdraw all day, every day. So every exchange keeps a smaller pile in hot wallets. Online.

Connected. Ready to pay out in seconds. A hot wallet doesn't move money on its own. Every withdrawal has to be signed by the exchange's signing service.

That's the real lock. Whoever controls the signing controls the money. And that service is built and run by engineers. Engineers with laptops.

Engineers with profiles on job sites. Which is exactly where the attackers went looking.

1:56 The Assignment: the fake job interview

On November third, Binance published its first account of what happened. It opens with an admission most companies never make. This should not have happened." According to Binance, the attackers first tried to get in back in June. That time, Binance detected the attempt and stopped it.

But the attackers learned something about how Binance worked. And they came back. In September, they found a software engineer on a legitimate job-hunting platform. His profile mentioned his experience at Binance.

To the attackers, that one line was a door. Someone posing as a project director reached out, from a company that turned out to be fictitious. Then came the interviews. First, a live video call with a supposed HR representative, who shared a social media account full of personal photos and videos.

Binance later called the approach" more advanced and deceptive" than anything it had seen before. The second interview was with the supposed CEO, in a popular chat app. According to NPR's account of Binance's findings, the engineer was on his work laptop. The CEO asked him to download a coding assignment, from an online code repository.

Here's what that assignment really did. Binance says the code dropped a new file into the Mac's Homebrew folder, called pie-env, with an underscore. Then it deleted the real pie-env, a normal tool many developers use, renamed the fake one to take its place, and used it to open a command shell. From that moment, a trusted tool on the engineer's laptop was quietly taking orders from a server the attackers controlled.

It was brand new malware. Binance calls it a zero-day. Its endpoint security didn't detect it. And the attackers worked to erase their tracks as they went.

They didn't rush. By October twentieth, they had used that laptop, and an internal AI tool, to break into the computers of two more employees. Both worked on Binance's wallet infrastructure. From them, the attackers took credentials, and a token that let them deploy code.

If you work in tech, this might sound familiar. Fake job interviews that end with malware are a signature of North Korean hacking campaigns, tracked as Contagious Interview and Operation Dream Job. In June twenty twenty-five, the security firm Socket reported one of these campaigns impersonating recruiters on LinkedIn and sending developers coding assignments laced with thirty-five malicious packages. Binance hasn't tied its breach to a specific campaign.

But the pattern is the same. A fake recruiter. Multiple interviews. A poisoned assignment.

By late October, the attackers had everything they needed. Now they just had to wait for the right night.

4:26 Twenty Minutes: the heist

Blockchain investigators at TRM Labs later reconstructed the operation almost minute by minute. On the morning of October twenty-eighth, the attackers made three small withdrawals from Tornado Cash, a crypto mixer that hides where money came from. That money funded the wallet that would run the heist. Early on October twenty-ninth, they deployed their attack code and tested it on two wallets of their own.

Then they rehearsed the final version on a third, and sent the test money back. About four hours later, the code was on a Binance hot wallet. Using the stolen credentials, they had reached the signing service itself. At five past eight in the evening, UTC, that's just after four in the afternoon in New York, five Binance wallets on the XRP Ledger sent two hundred thirty-three million XRP to a single address.

Then Binance wallets on Solana and Optimism followed. At eight twenty, everything happened at once. A Binance Bitcoin hot wallet sent eighteen thousand two hundred seventy bitcoin. Within two minutes, a Tron wallet sent over six hundred million dollars in Tether.

And on Ethereum, the attackers submitted one single transaction. Inside that transaction were fifty authorizations, signed in advance. They used a feature added to Ethereum in the Pectra upgrade, in May twenty twenty-five, called EIP seventy-seven-oh-two. It lets a normal wallet hand control to a smart contract.

Every wallet keeps a running counter of its transactions, and the attackers couldn't know exactly where Binance's counter would be at that moment. So they signed fifty, for fifty numbers in a row. One of them was bound to match. It did.

Three minutes and twenty-four seconds later, Binance's main Ethereum hot wallet was empty. One hundred sixty-seven thousand ether. Sixty-three other tokens. Including one point six billion dollars in Tether.

And from a separate Binance account that relied on the same wallet, another eight hundred twenty-three million in USDC. And the trap stayed open. The wallet now obeyed the attackers' contract, so new money that arrived was swept out again. Even after Binance suspended withdrawals.

Eight forty-one. Eight fifty-nine. Nine-oh-four. All the way to four minutes before midnight.

Just after eleven, a second wave hit Binance wallets on Avalanche and Polygon. When TRM added it up, around six point three six billion dollars had left Binance, across ten blockchains. Ethereum alone, almost three point eight billion. Bitcoin, nearly one point five billion.

Most of it in about twenty minutes. The previous record, the Bybit hack in twenty twenty-five, was one point five billion. This was four times bigger. And one detail tells you how deep the attackers really were.

On nine other blockchains, Binance wallets didn't need any trick at all. They sent money through ordinary, properly signed transfers. A second Ethereum hot wallet sent twenty-eight thousand ether the same way, with no special authorization. TRM's conclusion: the attackers likely controlled Binance's hot wallet signing system itself.

The lock wasn't picked. They were holding the key.

7:29 The Race to freeze the money

But the attackers had a problem. A huge part of what they stole could be taken back. Stablecoins like Tether and USDC are digital dollars issued by companies. And those companies can freeze them, at any address, at any time.

At nine-oh-one, Tether froze nine hundred seventy-one million dollars sitting in the attackers' main receiving wallet. Three minutes later, Circle froze eight hundred twenty-three million, in coordination with law enforcement. Ripple froze its stablecoin. Then World Liberty Financial.

And after midnight, the security council of the Arbitrum network forced about thirty million dollars back out of the attackers' hands. By November third, Binance said about two point eight five billion dollars had been frozen or intercepted. In a system built to be unstoppable, a handful of companies stopped almost half the heist. Bitcoin is different.

There's no company behind it, and nobody can freeze it. The eighteen thousand two hundred seventy bitcoin started moving through what investigators call a peel chain, broken into pieces of one thousand, and one hundred. Then the attackers did something that shows how much pressure they were under. They raced to turn freezable dollars into ether, which nobody can freeze.

According to Chainalysis, in about twenty minutes they swapped three hundred twenty-five million dollars in stablecoins for ether worth two hundred eighty-five million. They threw away forty million dollars, just to get out fast. They also deposited sixty million dollars in Tether on the lending platform Ah-vay, and borrowed nearly twenty thousand ether against it, before Ah-vay froze the affected markets. Chainalysis also saw thousands of transactions in a very short time, a sign the laundering was automated.

On the Tron network, many fresh addresses first received exactly one TRX, then a larger amount, then sent it onward. A machine, not a person, was moving the money. A little after midnight, Binance cut the attackers' access to its hot wallet. The night was over.

The hunt was just beginning.

9:28 The Trace: how it was linked to North Korea

Investigators look for fingerprints. And this attack had familiar ones. Small withdrawals from Tornado Cash to fund an operator. Careful staging, well before the theft.

Signatures collected in advance, then fired in one burst. TRM said, with high confidence, that this was TraderTraitor, North Korea's premier crypto hacking unit. Chainalysis found something even more direct. In real time, it watched money stolen from Binance mix with money stolen from another exchange, Bitget, just one month earlier.

On September twenty-fourth, Bitget had lost about three hundred eighty-seven million dollars, in a hack its own CEO linked to North Korea. Same launderers. Same wallets. Same hands.

Chainalysis published its attribution at half past midnight, Eastern time. Less than nine hours after the first transfer. Two days after the heist, the FBI made it official. In an alert titled" North Korea Responsible for six billion dollars plus Binance Attack," the Bureau said the Democratic People's Republic of Korea was responsible for stealing more than six billion dollars from Binance, and gave the activity a name.

TraderTraitor. Security researchers also track the group as Lazarus, Jade Sleet, and APT thirty-eight. Working with the Secret Service and IRS Criminal Investigation, the FBI said the stolen assets were being converted mostly into ether, and were expected to be laundered further through dee-fye and instant swap services. Then it published the addresses.

Six hundred and eight on Ethereum. Nineteen on Bitcoin. One on Solana. Six hundred twenty-eight wallets.

And a request to every exchange, node operator, and dee-fye service: block them. North Korea denies it. On November third, Kim Yo Jong, the sister of Kim Jong Un, accused the United States of using the hack to damage North Korea's reputation. There have been no arrests.

Investigators believe the people behind TraderTraitor work for a state that will never hand them over. What investigators can do is follow the money, and make every step of laundering slower, riskier, and more expensive. Turning stolen crypto into cash can take weeks. Sometimes years.

11:38 The Playbook: this wasn't the first time

And when we dug into the FBI's archive, we found something that changes how you see this story. Binance wasn't the first time. It wasn't even the second. In March twenty twenty-four, according to the FBI, a North Korean hacker posing as a recruiter on LinkedIn contacted an employee of Ginco, a Japanese company that makes crypto wallet software.

The employee had access to Ginco's wallet management system. The recruiter sent a link to a Python script on GitHub, described as a pre-employment test. The employee copied the code. And was compromised.

Two months later, the hackers used stolen session cookies to impersonate that employee, and manipulated a real transaction request from the Japanese exchange DMM. Four thousand five hundred and two bitcoin were gone. Three hundred eight million dollars. The FBI, together with Japan's National Police Agency, named the group responsible.

TraderTraitor. Then, in February twenty twenty-five, the exchange Bybit lost one point five billion dollars. Bybit's funds sat behind a wallet service called Safe. And according to Safe, the attack started when one of its developers' computers was compromised, after it interacted with a malicious Docker project.

Not a broken lock. A person, and a piece of code they trusted. The FBI named the same group. TraderTraitor.

Line it up, and the playbook is obvious. March twenty twenty-four: a fake recruiter, three hundred eight million. September twenty twenty-four: the FBI warns the entire industry. February twenty twenty-five: a compromised developer, one point five billion.

September twenty twenty-six: Bitget. And October twenty twenty-six: a fake job interview, six point three billion. Blockchain firm Chainalysis estimated that North Korea stole about two billion dollars in crypto in twenty twenty-five alone, a record at the time. The Binance heist, in a single night, was three times that.

Why? The FBI says it plainly. North Korea uses cybercrime and cryptocurrency theft, quote," to generate revenue for the regime." And jobs are a weapon in both directions. Two weeks before the Binance heist, the U.S.

Justice Department announced a crackdown on a different North Korean scheme. Instead of fake recruiters, fake employees. According to the indictment, from twenty nineteen until the summer of twenty twenty-five, North Korean IT workers used stolen or fabricated identities, and VP-Ns, to look like Americans working from home. They got remote jobs at more than seventy U.S. companies, in health care, communications, cyber support, retail, and manufacturing, across seventeen states and Washington, DC.

The company laptops were shipped to helpers inside the United States, who kept them running in their homes, installed remote-access software, and sent the paychecks on to accounts controlled by North Korea. In Texas, over five days in September, the FBI searched more than forty homes suspected of being these" laptop farms." They called it Operation Lonestar Roundup. Fourteen people were charged. About one hundred eighty-seven laptops and devices were seized.

The security firm CrowdStrike tracks this group under another name: Famous Chollima. The FBI's cyber division put it this way. North Korea uses these schemes to" generate revenue for the regime and its weapons programs." A fake job offer in one direction. A fake job applicant in the other.

Both end in the same place.

14:59 Protect Yourself: what the FBI says to do

For Binance's customers, the story ended better than it could have. Binance covered the losses with its own money, including its emergency fund, and said ninety-eight percent of the affected tokens were replaced by October thirty-first. Withdrawals reopened on November first. In the first nine hours, users pulled out three point six billion dollars more than they deposited.

Bitcoin fell about five percent. But customers were made whole. This time. Binance says it has rebuilt the affected systems, replaced the compromised devices, revoked the stolen credentials, and brought in independent security firms to review what happened.

But the weak point in this story wasn't a server. It was a moment of trust between two people. And that's something no exchange can patch for you. Now, here's the part that matters for you.

This trick doesn't need a crypto exchange. It needs a person who's looking for a job. Maybe you. In September twenty twenty-four, the FBI published exactly what to watch for.

The FBI warned that North Korean hackers study their targets on professional networking and job sites, build trust over long conversations, and then ask for a" pre-employment test" that means running unknown code, scripts, or GitHub repositories. The warning goes further. These hackers usually write in fluent, or nearly fluent, English. They know the technical side of crypto.

They may pretend to be someone you actually know, using real photos stolen from social media. And they build fake recruiting firms and tech companies, with professional-looking websites. The Justice Department once seized seventeen of those fake domains in a single case. So a polished website proves nothing.

Here's what the FBI says to do. One. Never run an interview assignment on your work computer, or the computer you use for everything else. If a test really requires running code, the FBI says: use a virtual machine, on a separate device, or on a device the company provides.

Two. Verify the recruiter through a separate channel, like a live video call on a different app. And watch for the red flags the FBI lists. An unexpected offer from a big company with unrealistically high pay.

Pressure to move the conversation to another messenger. Or a request to install custom software, just to join a call. Three. If you own crypto, never keep your seed phrase, private keys, or wallet passwords on a device that's connected to the internet.

And if you hire people remotely, the same warning runs the other way. The FBI has published guidance for companies on spotting North Korean IT workers. Verify who you're actually hiring. If you think you already ran something you shouldn't have: disconnect that device from the internet, but don't turn it off.

That keeps the evidence. Then report it at IC-three dot gov. In March twenty twenty-four, a fake recruiter cost three hundred eight million dollars. In September twenty twenty-four, the FBI published a warning about it.

Publicly. For free. Two years later, the same trick cost six point three billion. If this helped you, send it to someone who's looking for a job right now.

It might be the most valuable link they get this year. Next time: the hackers who say they went after the FBI itself.

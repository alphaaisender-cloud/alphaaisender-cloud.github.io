القضية

لماذا تصلني فجأة كل هذه الرسائل النصية المزعجة؟ جزء من الإجابة: تلك الرسالة عن رسوم طريق غير مدفوعة أو طرد أو «تنبيه احتيال» ربما لم تأتِ من بنكك — ولا حتى من شركة الاتصالات. سحبت الشرطة في ست دول الجهاز نفسه من سيارات: جهاز إرسال رسائل جماعي (SMS blaster)، أي برج اتصالات مزيف في صندوق السيارة يرسل الرسائل إلى آلاف الهواتف دفعة واحدة. تتبّعنا القضايا الحقيقية — طوكيو، لندن، تورونتو، أثينا، جنيف، ماليزيا — ونشرح كيف توقف الرسائل المزعجة على آيفون وأندرويد، بما في ذلك الإعداد الوحيد الذي يجعل هاتفك يتجاهل البرج المزيف.

كيف توقف الرسائل المزعجة (آيفون، أندرويد، سامسونج)

  1. لا تطلب منك أي شركة طرق أو بنك أو شركة توصيل الدفع عبر رابط في رسالة نصية. افتح تطبيقها أو موقعها بنفسك.
  2. لا ترد على الرسائل المجهولة. احظر الرقم (سامسونج: افتح الرسالة ← ⋮ ← حظر الرقم؛ آيفون: اضغط على المرسل ← حظر هذا المتصل ← الإبلاغ عن رسالة غير مرغوبة).
  3. أعد توجيه رسائل الاحتيال إلى 7726 (الولايات المتحدة، كندا، المملكة المتحدة)، ثم احذفها.
  4. أبلغ عبر ReportFraud.ftc.gov أو ic3.gov.
  5. أوقف شبكة 2G إن سمح هاتفك (أندرويد: الإعدادات ← الاتصالات ← شبكات الجوال ← السماح بـ 2G؛ آيفون: وضع القفل — يقيّد بعض التطبيقات أيضًا).
  6. بنكك الحقيقي لن يطلب منك أبدًا نقل أموالك «لحمايتها»، ولن يتصل بك ليسألك عن رمز أرسله لك للتو.

أسئلة وأجوبة

لماذا كثرت الرسائل المزعجة مؤخرًا؟

ارتفعت الخسائر المبلّغ عنها في الولايات المتحدة من احتيال الرسائل النصية من 131 مليون دولار (2021) إلى 470 مليونًا (2024، FTC). ترسل حزم «التصيّد كخدمة» ملايين الرسائل، غالبًا عبر iMessage/RCS التي لا تراها فلاتر شركات الاتصالات — وبعضها يأتي من أجهزة الإرسال الجماعي: أبراج مزيفة تتنقل داخل السيارات.

ما هو جهاز الإرسال الجماعي (SMS blaster)؟

برج اتصالات مزيف ومحمول. يُجبر الهواتف القريبة على النزول إلى شبكة 2G، حيث لا يتحقق الهاتف من صحة البرج، ثم يرسل رسائل بأي اسم مرسل. صادرت الشرطة هذه الأجهزة في المملكة المتحدة وكندا واليابان واليونان وسويسرا وماليزيا.

هل يمكنني اكتشاف برج مزيف بالقرب مني؟

عادةً لا. الحماية: أوقف شبكة 2G ولا تدفع أبدًا عبر رابط في رسالة نصية.

هل رسالة رسوم الطريق حقيقية؟

تقريبًا لا. حذّر مكتب التحقيقات الفيدرالي من رسالة «رسوم طريق مستحقة بقيمة 12.51 دولارًا».

«آسف، رقم خاطئ»؟

لا ترد — ولا حتى بكلمة STOP. يصفها مكتب التحقيقات الفيدرالي بأنها حيلة تقود إلى استثمارات مشفرة مزيفة.

Why are you getting so many spam texts lately? Here's part of the answer. Right now, a car could be parked outside your home, or the mall you're in. In its trunk, a box that pretends to be a cell tower.

Your phone connects to it without asking you. And then you get a text built to take your money. Americans reported losing four hundred seventy million dollars to scam texts in a single year. And that's only what people reported.

Police have now pulled these machines out of cars in six countries. You'll see the real evidence: police video, court records, FBI warnings. Everyone tells you, "don't click the link." We'll show you the one setting that makes a fake tower useless. November seventh, twenty twenty-six.

Saturday. Ginza, the most expensive shopping street in Tokyo. A car is parked at the curb. Around it, thousands of phones suddenly lose their signal.

And then, some of them get a text. Your card payment has been suspended. Tap the link to fix it. Tokyo police say the texts didn't come from a bank, and didn't even come through a phone company.

They came from inside that car. Officers arrested a thirty-one-year-old man on the spot, on suspicion of running an illegal cell tower out of the vehicle. According to Japanese media, in one or two hours, the device knocked more than ten thousand phones off their real network. He denies it.

It's the first case like this in Japan. But not in the world. In the last eighteen months, police have pulled the same kind of machine out of cars in London, Geneva, Athens, Malaysia, and Toronto. The FTC says people often lose tens of thousands of dollars that way.

7:13How a fake cell tower beats your carrier's spam filter

Now back to the cars. Some criminals decided they didn't need the internet, or a phone farm, at all. They bought a cell tower. Police call the device an SMS blaster.

Your phone is always looking for the strongest cell tower nearby. An SMS blaster simply shouts louder than the real one. And your phone switches to it. There's a trick inside.

The blaster pushes your phone down to the old 2G network, a standard from the nineteen nineties. On 2G, your phone has to prove who it is to the tower, but the tower never has to prove anything to your phone. So your phone can't tell a real tower from a box in a trunk. And you'd never notice.

Your signal flickers for a moment, the text arrives, and your phone goes back to the real network as if nothing happened. The message just sits in your inbox, looking like every other text from your bank. Once your phone is connected, the blaster can send it anything, with any sender name it likes. Your bank.

The tax office. The post office. In the words of London police, criminals can bypass the safeguards designed to block malicious sender names and harmful links. The message never touches your carrier's network, so its filter never sees it.

And here's the part that answers another question people keep asking: how did they get my number? With a blaster, they don't need it. They don't text you. They text everyone around them.

You were just standing in the wrong place. Here's what shocked me the most. For years we've been teaching each other: don't give your number to strangers, double-check who's calling you, who's texting you. But here, your number isn't needed at all.

Picture this: you're just standing at a bus STOP. A car is parked nearby, and inside it there's a device, at first glance just a box, that automatically sends you messages. You think you're talking to your bank, but these messages never even reach your carrier.

8:57London: the SMS blaster that texted the police

March twenty twenty-five. London. A student from China named Ruichen Xiong has run up serious gambling debts. Someone offers him a way to pay them off.

All he has to do is drive. For six days, he drives a black Honda around London with an SMS blaster in the trunk, following routes and timings he's been given. Tens of thousands of phones in range. And then he makes a mistake no script could plan for.

He drives right past police officers. Their phones buzz. A text from HMRC, Britain's tax office. Except the officers know it isn't.

The blaster had just texted the police themselves. They STOP the car with the device still running in the back. That summer, Xiong is sentenced to more than a year in prison. But a driver isn't a boss.

Britain's specialist card fraud unit kept pulling the thread. Across its blaster investigations, it arrested seven people and seized seven of these devices. And in this case, the thread led to a man named Di Li. Police say Li, forty-three, from Finchley, arranged the device, helped get the car, and paid Xiong 's living costs.

The routes were coordinated remotely, by Li and others in a wider network. Police describe those drives as a" testing" phase. A test run, in the middle of London. Officers searched his home in August twenty twenty-five and found his messages with Xiong about the blaster.

He was arrested on September first. In court, Li had an explanation. The machine, he said, was for advertising. He was just a middleman for someone in China, trying to help a young man pay off his debts.

The jury didn't buy it. In June twenty twenty-six, he was sentenced to forty-eight months. And Xiong wasn't even the first. Back in May twenty twenty-four, police found a man sitting in a van in the car park of the Trafford Centre, a huge shopping mall near Manchester.

Hidden in wooden compartments in the back was an active SMS blaster, with aerials on the roof. Authorities called it the first one they had ever found in Britain. His texts pretended to be from Royal Mail. And on his phones, police found seven thousand, eight hundred and fifty-nine stolen card numbers.

In August twenty twenty-six, Mohammed Faiyaz Iqbal, forty-three, was sentenced to three years and eight months. The detective on the case gave one piece of advice, and it works in the U.S., Canada and the U.K. too. If you get a suspicious text, don't reply. Forward it to 7726.

On your keypad, that spells SPAM. Your carrier uses those reports to block the next wave.

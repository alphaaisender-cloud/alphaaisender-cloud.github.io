Дело

A photo of the front. A photo of the back. An infrared layer. An ultraviolet layer.

And that timestamp. Down to the second. A year later, that exact scan is for sale on a Russian-speaking hacker forum. Along with more than one hundred fifty-three million others.

Drivers from the United States and Canada. The FBI opened an investigation. Lawsuits followed. And most of the people in that database?

Nobody told them a thing. This is the story of what happens to your data after a breach. Where it goes. Who buys it.

1:05The Sample: 153 million licenses for sale

On August thirty-first, twenty twenty-six, a new seller appeared on Exploit, a Russian-language cybercrime forum. The offer: scans of identity documents for more than one hundred seventy million people in North America. The service was called Nexus. To prove the data was real, the seller posted a free sample.

A Virginia driver's license. It belonged to Brian Krebs, one of the best-known cybersecurity journalists in America. Krebs looked inside. More than one hundred fifty-three million driver's licenses.

More than ten million ID cards. Three million travel documents. Over half a million medical cards. A blank search returned around eleven and a half million pages of results.

Most of the records were American. But about one point one million came from Canada. The largest share, nearly four hundred seventy-four thousand, from Ontario. And it was growing.

In a single day, the number of licenses went up by almost four hundred thousand. The sellers bragged they had been, quote," continuously exfiltrating new data for over a year." So where did it come from? Krebs noticed something about the timestamps. His license had been scanned on a day in June twenty twenty-five, when he flew to a family funeral.

His mother's license was in the database too. Scanned a few seconds after his. There was only one place they had both handed over their licenses that day. Together.

At the same moment. The rental car counter. Krebs asked friends and family to search for their own licenses. Nine found them.

Every one of them had been traveling on, or near, the date in their timestamp. Several had rented cars. A privacy researcher found his license stamped in the middle of a trip to Las Vegas. The only place he was sure his ID went into a machine that day was a cannabis dispensary.

The rental chain and the dispensary had something in common. A Louisiana company called IDScan dot net. It sells machines and software that scan IDs, with infrared and ultraviolet light, to check that they're real. It says it runs more than twenty-one million verifications a month, at more than twenty thousand locations.

On September first, the FBI's New Orleans field office opened an investigation. A week later, IDScan published a notice. An unauthorized third party may have accessed and copied customer information, including names and license numbers. At least eight class-action lawsuits have been filed.

The company has not been found liable for anything. And Nexus? It went offline, right after the story came out. The storefront disappeared.

The data didn't. If you think big organizations notice quickly, consider the Pentagon. This September, the Defense Manpower Data Center sent letters to about three million people. Names.

Dates of birth. Social Security numbers. Someone had been inside its system from October twenty twenty-five until July twenty twenty-six. Nine months.

Nobody noticed. So now your file has a name, a face, an address, a date of birth, and maybe a Social Security number. The next question is simple. How much is it worth?

4:08The Market: how your data is sold

The dark web doesn't look like a horror movie. It looks like online shopping. Nexus let buyers preview a record before they paid, with the sensitive parts blurred. There are search bars.

Seller ratings. Reviews. Customer support. And the prices are lower than you'd think.

According to the threat-intelligence firm SOCRadar, a US Social Security number sells for one to six dollars. A scan of an ID or a driver's license, thirty to sixty. A full identity package, name, Social Security number, date of birth, often called" fullz," maybe twenty to a hundred. Your Social Security number can cost less than a cup of coffee.

The damage it can do does not. The worst part isn't the price. The worst part is that the same database goes to dozens of customers. And each of them tries their own tools to use your personal data.

Some markets sell something even more valuable than documents. Genesis Market sold entire digital lives. Since twenty eighteen, according to the Justice Department, it offered data stolen from more than one and a half million infected computers. More than eighty million account logins.

Plus browser cookies and device fingerprints, so a criminal could log in looking exactly like you. In April twenty twenty-three, the FBI and Dutch police took it down, in an operation with a name you won't forget. Operation Cookie Monster. Seventeen countries.

One hundred nineteen arrests. But here's the pattern. When one market dies, another one opens. When RaidForums was seized, BreachForums took its place.

Its founder was arrested, and later sentenced to three years in prison. The FBI seized the site. It came back. In twenty twenty-five, one of its best-known figures, known as IntelBroker, was arrested in France and charged in New York.

That October, the FBI and French authorities seized the site's domain yet again. And the criminals fight over this market as hard as the police fight them. Rival groups accuse each other of working for law enforcement. In twenty twenty-five, one of the most notorious crews publicly claimed that Breach Forums itself had become a police trap.

Nobody trusts anybody. Except the buyers. They keep coming back. So who are those buyers?

And what do they actually do with a file like yours?

6:24The Paperwork: what they do with your ID

The first thing is the simplest. With your name, date of birth, Social Security number and a picture of your license, someone can try to open credit in your name. A card. A loan.

A phone contract. You find out when the bill, or the debt collector, finds you. The second is your tax refund. In twenty twenty-five, the FBI warned that criminals were stealing taxpayer identities to file fake returns and claim the refund first.

Complaints were up twenty-six percent in a year. You file in April. The IRS tells you: you already did. The third is your phone number.

It's called SIM swapping. A criminal calls your carrier, pretends to be you, using exactly the details we just saw for sale, and moves your number to their SIM card. Now every" forgot your password" code goes to them. In twenty twenty-one alone, the FBI received sixteen hundred complaints, with more than sixty-eight million dollars lost.

How easy is it? Ask the US Securities and Exchange Commission. In January twenty twenty-four, a man from Alabama printed a fake ID on a portable card printer, walked into a phone store, and talked his way into a new SIM card for the phone number behind the SEC's account on X. Minutes later, his partners posted a fake announcement in the regulator's name.

The price of bitcoin jumped more than a thousand dollars. One printed ID. In May twenty twenty-five, he was sentenced to fourteen months in prison. And the fourth is new.

The FBI says criminals now use AI to generate fake identification documents, including driver's licenses. A real scan of your license is the perfect template. Add it all up, and the FBI's latest report counts more than one million complaints, and nearly twenty-one billion dollars in losses, in a single year. That's almost three thousand complaints every day.

But your documents are only half the file. The other half is on your computer. And you probably let it in yourself.

8:17The Vacuum: infostealers and cookies

It usually starts with something free. A cracked program. A game cheat. A pop-up that says your browser needs an update.

You click. Nothing seems to happen. But in the background, a program called an infostealer is running. It doesn't lock your files.

It doesn't ask for ransom. It just vacuums. Saved passwords. Autofill.

Credit cards stored in your browser. Crypto wallet files. And your cookies. Then it deletes itself, and you never see it again.

One of the most popular was called Lumma. The FBI said it had identified at least one point seven million cases where Lumma was used to steal information like this. In May twenty twenty-five, the Justice Department seized the domains of its control panels, and Microsoft went to court to take down about twenty-three hundred more. By early twenty twenty-six, security researchers reported it was back.

A lot of people think: I changed my password, so I'm safe. No. If they have your cookies, they don't need your password at all. The FBI's Atlanta office has warned about exactly this.

When you tick" remember me," the site gives your browser a cookie, often good for about thirty days. Steal that cookie, and you can walk into someone's email without the password. Without the two-factor code. The site thinks it's you.

That's why fresh stolen logs are the expensive ones. SOCRadar says a log less than forty-eight hours old can sell for several times more. Because the cookies still work. And here's the part most people never think about.

They don't have to use it right away. In twenty twenty-two, attackers stole encrypted password vaults from LastPass. Some of those vaults held the keys to crypto wallets. The thefts came later.

Months later. Years later. In one federal case, investigators say a victim lost about one hundred fifty million dollars after keys stored in a hacked password manager were stolen. In March twenty twenty-five, they seized more than twenty-three million.

LastPass says it hasn't seen conclusive evidence linking the thefts to its breach. Stolen data doesn't expire. It waits. Sometimes until there's more money to take.

There's one thing left in your file. And it's the one that reaches your family.

10:26The Voice: AI clones and fake police

Your phone rings in the evening. You pick up. Nobody says anything. Then they hang up.

You've probably heard the rumor: they're recording your voice, so an AI can copy it. I checked that rumor. Neither the FBI nor the Federal Trade Commission says silent calls record your voice. Most of the time, they're just checking whether your number is real.

But the truth is worse. They don't need your call. Finding your social media is enough to carry out their plan. The FBI's warning is direct.

Criminals generate short audio clips of a loved one's voice, to impersonate a relative in a crisis and ask for money. Or a ransom. This is not hypothetical. In July twenty twenty-five, in Dover, Florida, Sharon Brightwell picked up a call from a number that looked like her daughter's.

She heard her daughter crying. A car crash. Then a man who said he was her lawyer asked for bail money. She withdrew fifteen thousand dollars and handed it to a courier.

Her daughter was safe at work the whole time. Brightwell believes the voice was cloned with AI, from clips on social media. The sheriff's office opened an investigation. It was her grandson who finally called the real number.

In December twenty twenty-five, the FBI described another version. Virtual kidnapping. Criminals take photos from social media, alter them into fake proof of life, and send them with timers, so you can't look closely before they disappear. Then there's the call from the police.

Your son was in an accident. He's in trouble. Pay now and the officer can make it go away. Don't tell anyone.

Stay on the line. Between January twenty twenty-five and July twenty twenty-six, the FBI received nearly sixty-one thousand complaints about scammers impersonating law enforcement and government officials. More than one point six billion dollars lost. The FBI lists the signs.

They keep you on the phone. They tell you not to talk to your family. They want crypto, gift cards, a courier, or cash in a crypto ATM. Some now appear on video calls as fake officers, made with AI.

And sometimes they don't fake your voice at all. They take over your messaging account with a single verification code, then write to everyone in your contacts. As you. Police in the UK and Singapore have warned about exactly this.

There's one defense better than any software, and it costs zero dollars. Zero. Agree on a special word with your family. Today.

If your child calls you from the police and doesn't know that word, it's not your son. Name. Face. Address.

Date of birth. Social Security number. Phone number. Passwords.

Cookies. Voice. That's the whole file. Now let's make it useless.

13:13Lock It Down: 7 steps from the FBI and IRS

One. Freeze your credit. In the US, it's free at all three bureaus, Equifax, Experian and TransUnion, and it stops anyone from opening new credit in your name. You can unfreeze it any time.

In Canada, a free freeze, or credit lock, is available in Quebec, and since July twenty twenty-six, in Ontario. Everywhere else, ask the credit bureaus for a fraud alert on your file. Two. If you're in the US, get an Identity Protection PIN from the IRS.

It's a six-digit code that blocks anyone else from filing a tax return with your Social Security number. The FBI recommends it. Three. Call your mobile carrier and ask for a port-out PIN, or number lock.

It makes a SIM swap much harder. Four. Agree on a family code word. That's straight from the FBI.

Five. On your email and your bank, skip" remember me," sign out when you're done, and check which devices are logged in. Never keep a crypto seed phrase on a computer or a phone. Not in a note.

Not in a screenshot. Six. Real police and government agencies will never call you to demand payment. Not in crypto.

Not in gift cards. Not by courier. Hang up, and call the agency back on a number you find yourself. Seven.

Find out where you've already leaked. Have I Been Pwned is a free site where you can check your email address. When the FBI took down Genesis Market, it handed the stolen logins to that very site, so victims could find out. And if something happens, report it.

In the US, at IC-3 dot gov and IdentityTheft dot gov. In Canada, to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre. Pause this video. Check your email.

It takes thirty seconds. You can change a password. You can reissue a card. But you can't change your date of birth.

You can't change your face. But you can make your data very hard to use. Fourteen thirty-two and seven seconds. Somewhere, a copy of that moment is still out there.

You can't get it back. But now, when someone tries to use it, the door is locked. One of the crews fighting over this market calls itself ShinyHunters. In May twenty twenty-six, the FBI warned that this group steals millions of records at a time, and then calls and texts victims and their families to pressure them into paying.

That's our next investigation.

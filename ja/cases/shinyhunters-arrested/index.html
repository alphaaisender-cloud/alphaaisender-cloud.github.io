事件

2026年5月、ハッカー集団ShinyHuntersは、数百の学校のCanvasログインページを身代金要求文に差し替えました。FBIは一般向けに警告を出しました。9月、集団はFBIの採用サイトFBIJobs.govをハッキングして応酬し、5,000人以上のFBI職員のデータを入手したと主張しました。するとFBIはカメラの前で彼らに語りかけました：「お前たちは我々の見つけ方を知っている。我々もお前たちの見つけ方を知っている」

この調査の内容

  • ShinyHuntersがCanvasとPowerSchoolを襲い、数千万人の生徒のデータを奪った経緯
  • Google、Cisco、カンタス航空、アディダス、シャネルに侵入した電話の手口（「こちらIT部門です…」）
  • FBIJobs.govのハッキング：セキュリティパッチを入れていなかった委託業者
  • アムステルダム：Signalにログインしたまま逮捕されたリーダーとされる人物
  • 名前を引き継いだヨルダンの10代「Rey」——拘束され、FBIに協力していると報じられる
  • 米国で起訴されたランサムウェア交渉人（無罪と推定）
  • ShinyHuntersが今や誰でも名乗れる「ブランド」になった理由
  • FBIが勧める、あなたと家族を守る7つのステップ

7ステップの対策（FBIとIC3の助言より）

  1. 脅迫には払わず、応じないでください。支払っても沈黙は買えません。
  2. 緊急の電話は、すでに知っている番号で確認してください。まず電話を切って。
  3. すべて保存し（ユーザー名、メール、番号、スクリーンショット）、通報してください：ic3.gov（米国）、Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre（カナダ）。
  4. 「スワッティング」で脅された場合は、事前に地元の警察に知らせてください。
  5. haveibeenpwned.com でメールアドレスを確認し、使い回しのパスワードを変更し、2段階認証をオンにし、クレジットフリーズも検討してください。
  6. 職場で：「ITサポート」が接続の承認やコードの読み上げを求めてきたら？電話を切り、自分でIT部門に電話してください。
  7. 学校のデータが漏れた？学籍番号を知っているフィッシングに備えてください。クリックする前に確認を。
  8. おまけ：今夜、家族の合言葉を決めましょう。

0:00They hacked the FBI

September fifteenth, twenty twenty-six. Amsterdam, evening. Dutch police storm a home. The man they want is twenty-four.

When they take him, he's still logged in to his Signal messenger, his chats open on the screen. One week later, the hacking group the FBI says he helped lead announces it has broken into the FBI itself. And the FBI answers, on camera. The longer you stay in this, the more we learn about you.

You know how to find us and we know how to find you. This is the story of how they did it. And of how the FBI hunted them down, across three countries, in less than a month. And of the one arrest nobody saw coming: a man whose job was to protect the victims.

It started in a classroom. May seventh, twenty twenty-six. Final exams. Students across America open Canvas, the platform where thousands of schools keep their classes.

Instead of their coursework, they see a note. ShinyHunters has breached Instructure. Again." The login pages of around three hundred thirty schools and universities had been replaced with a ransom demand. The hackers claimed data on two hundred seventy-five million students and teachers, and told each school to come and negotiate, before May twelfth.

Instructure took Canvas offline and called it" scheduled maintenance." Four days later, it announced an agreement with the hackers. It never said what that agreement cost. The company said the stolen data included names, email addresses, student ID numbers, and messages between users. The hackers claimed billions of private messages between students and teachers.

And schools already knew this group. Months earlier, the University of Pennsylvania had refused a one-million-dollar demand from ShinyHunters. In March, its donor records and internal memos were published online. My name is Dimitri, and I run the Breach and Trace channel.

Picture this. Your kid is going into an exam. They open the school website, with their grades and all their coursework, and instead of the login page, there's a letter from hackers. Pay us, or we put all your data online.

In May, hundreds of schools across the country got that letter. And it was only the beginning.

2:10The FBI's warning

Eight days later, on May fifteenth, the FBI published a warning about the group. It said ShinyHunters doesn't stop at stealing data. To pressure victims, it sends threatening texts and makes phone calls, to the victims and to their families. And in some cases, it uses swatting: a fake emergency call that sends armed police to someone's home.

They often claim to have embarrassing photos or videos of their victims. The FBI says those frequently don't exist. Its advice was simple. Do not send payment.

Do not respond to their demands. So who are they? The name comes from a video game habit: hunting for rare, shiny Pokémon. The hunting started around twenty twenty.

Microsoft source code from a private GitHub account. Tens of millions of user records from apps like Wattpad and Animal Jam. Later came breaches linked to the same name at AT&T, Ticketmaster and the bank Santander. And PowerSchool, the student records company used by schools in the United States and Canada.

Data on sixty-two million students and nine and a half million teachers. A twenty-year-old college student from Massachusetts admitted his role in that hack, and got four years in federal prison. Years earlier, a young Frenchman tied to the group got three. Their favorite tool isn't code.

It's a phone call. Someone calls an employee, pretends to be IT support, and talks them into handing over access. In twenty twenty-five, that trick reached the customer databases of companies like Google, Cisco, Qantas, Adidas and Chanel. Here's how that call goes.

Hi, this is IT. We're fixing a problem with your account. Can you open this page and approve the connection?" The employee clicks approve. And without knowing it, they've just plugged the hackers' tool straight into the company's customer database.

No virus. No break-in. Just a polite voice on the phone. Later that year, using stolen access keys instead of phone calls, the group claimed it had pulled about one and a half billion records from seven hundred sixty companies.

And in March of twenty twenty-six, it hit Canada. The telecom giant Telus confirmed unauthorized access to some of its systems. ShinyHunters claimed nearly a petabyte of data, and demanded sixty-five million dollars. Later, the FBI put its own numbers on it, speaking about the man arrested in Amsterdam.

Since last year, this cyber criminal and his co -conspirators have allegedly breached more than 140 organizations and taken at least $70 million in extortion payments.

4:36The revenge: FBIJobs.gov

ShinyHunters took the FBI's warning personally. In June, the group told the tech site BleepingComputer it had already tried to break into one specific target: the FBI's own hiring system. That time, it failed. On September twenty-second, it tried again, and this time it posted a message addressed to the FBI's director.

We have compromised the FBI. We hold very sensitive data on almost all FBI agents and individuals who filed an application with the FBI for a job." The target was FBIJobs.gov, the bureau's recruiting website. According to reporting by Reuters and KrebsOnSecurity, the stolen files covered more than five thousand FBI personnel. Home addresses.

Social Security numbers. Each person's unit and specialization. Even medical and psychiatric records. The FBI confirmed it was investigating, and took the site offline.

The hackers said they didn't want money. They wanted the FBI to take back its May warning. Some veteran agents told CBS News this might be the worst breach of a federal government system since twenty fifteen, when hackers stole more than twenty-two million government personnel records. And the way in was painfully ordinary.

A known flaw in Oracle's PeopleSoft, a human resources system. The FBI later said a contractor had failed to install the security patch. The bureau removed the contractor. The FBI wasn't the only one.

Security researchers at Mandiant and Google found the same group had used the same PeopleSoft flaw against dozens of organizations: universities, healthcare, agriculture, transportation, and government. And it was real. The group sent NBC News a sample document about a former agent, and the agent confirmed it was authentic. Think about what that means.

The people whose job is to protect the country could now face the exact tactics the FBI warned about in May. Harassment. Extortion. Swatting.

The hackers had turned the warning back on its authors. Think about it. This wasn't a bank or an online store. It was an official FBI website.

And if they could get the agents' data that easily, do you really think our data is any problem for them? But that's not all. The FBI answered.

6:52The man in Amsterdam

Here's the strange part. By the time ShinyHunters announced the FBI hack, one of its alleged leaders had already been in a Dutch jail cell for a week. On the evening of September fifteenth, Dutch police raided a home in Amsterdam. Local media reported they used flash-bang grenades.

Their target was Pepijn van der Stap, twenty-four. According to a source quoted by KrebsOnSecurity, he was arrested while logged in to his Signal messenger, and his recent conversations were right there on the screen. He wasn't a stranger to police. Van der Stap had already served most of a four-year sentence for data theft and extortion.

Before that first arrest, he had worked as a software engineer at a cybersecurity startup, and volunteered at a Dutch institute that helps companies fix security holes. At the same time, prosecutors said, he was hacking and extorting companies. After prison, he presented himself as a reformed hacker. A Dutch security company hired him as its" offensive security lead," paid to probe client networks for weaknesses.

The company's owner later said investigators found no evidence he had acted against the firm or its clients. Six days before the raid, he told KrebsOnSecurity:" I'm doing what I can to repay victims, and that's all I can do." He is presumed innocent in the new case. ShinyHunters itself denied he was one of them. Thirteen days later, the FBI released a video about the arrest.

And it spoke directly to the rest of the group. Now, to the remaining members of ShinyHunters. You've heard about the arrest of your colleague. We're confident you've seen or heard things in recent days that the public has not.

Other groups believed anonymity or their friends would protect them and they were wrong. Arrests have a way of changing who is willing to talk and seized infrastructure has a way of showing us who's left.

8:42The king

With the Dutchman behind bars, someone else took over the ShinyHunters name. Online, he called himself" Rey," Spanish for king. According to KrebsOnSecurity, Rey was a teenager living in Amman, Jordan. Because he may be a minor, we won't name him or show his face.

Rey spent the next days taunting the FBI on social media. And he did something cold. In his memes, he kept using the avatar of the arrested Dutchman, a dark Pokémon called Umbreon. KrebsOnSecurity read it as an attempt to pin the hacks on a man who was already in a cell.

Rey also boasted about extorting another criminal gang, the ransomware group Cl0p. Criminals robbing criminals. This wasn't new for him: in a profile published the year before, KrebsOnSecurity reported that Rey admitted working with several ransomware groups. Shortly after Krebs reached out again in September, Rey began deleting his social media accounts.

At the same time, the group was allegedly extorting a company called Jeppesen ForeFlight, an aviation navigation business that Boeing had sold the year before for ten and a half billion dollars. Boeing said it was reviewing the claims. Jeppesen ForeFlight said there was no impact to its operations or products. Then it ended.

In early October, Reuters reported that Jordanian authorities had detained Rey, and that he was cooperating with the FBI. The king had started talking.

10:04The negotiator

Now the twist. In early October, people who sell protection against hackers gathered at a cyber insurance conference in a Philadelphia hotel. According to a source quoted by KrebsOnSecurity, a fifty-four-year-old Canadian executive from Toronto was in town for it. His business was ransomware negotiation: when a company gets hacked and blackmailed, firms like his talk to the criminals on the company's behalf.

He had even written a book about it, a two-hundred-fifty-two-page guide to cyber extortion. Its central idea: talking to a criminal is not the same as agreeing to pay. On October eighth, the FBI arrested him in Pennsylvania. His name is Edward Dubrovsky.

Court records show he was charged with conspiracy to threaten to release stolen data in order to extort money, and with interfering with commerce by threats. The core complaint is sealed. The case was moved to Texas, which sources describe as the center of the whole Shiny Hunters investigation. According to Politico, prosecutors asked that he stay in custody until trial.

He has not been convicted of anything. The FBI never publicly named him. Asked about his arrest, it pointed to a statement from its director about" the latest arrest this FBI has made in a matter of days involving this network." Canada's federal police said they were aware a Canadian had been arrested in the United States. What did investigators find?

Sources told KrebsOnSecurity that the chats seized in Amsterdam showed frequent conversations between the Dutch suspect and ransomware negotiators, and that some of them knew about new victims before the victims themselves did. Those are claims from sources, not findings of a court. Investigators were also looking at a website called Shinywire. From early May until mid-October, it listed nearly one hundred hacked organizations, each page with samples of stolen files and a clock: negotiate within seven days, or everything gets published.

Two sources told KrebsOnSecurity that the FBI believes at least one executive at a large American consulting firm helped run it. That person has not been named or charged. It exposed an uncomfortable truth about this industry. Some negotiators charge a percentage of the ransom.

The bigger the payment, the bigger their fee. No one in the ecosystem should be profiting purely on the size of the ransomware transaction," said Mark Camillo, who leads an industry group founded by cyber insurers. And this wasn't the only case. On October seventh, the Justice Department charged the head of another response firm with allegedly paying hackers eight million dollars while billing his clients nineteen million.

In July, a negotiator from Florida was sentenced to seventy months in prison for conspiring with the ransomware gang BlackCat. And there's a detail that tells you who the hackers really feared. In private chats from May, shared with KrebsOnSecurity, the group complained about a response firm that kept telling victims not to pay. Someone," they wrote," is going out of their way to make sure I am not paid." The people ShinyHunters hated most weren't the police.

They were the people telling victims not to pay. Here's what struck me most. The victims were asking for help. And the man helping them is accused of being part of the extortion himself.

No court has proven that, and until one does, he's presumed innocent. But I have a question. Who do you call when someone is blackmailing you?

13:26The name that won't die

So is ShinyHunters finished? Not quite. A researcher at the security firm Flare found that within a week of Rey's detention, rival channels were claiming the leadership. By mid-October, at least three factions each said they were the real ShinyHunters.

A new leak site went online. Publicly, they attacked each other. Quietly, they kept sending victims to the same download server. As the researcher put it:" The September arrests should have been the end of Shiny Hunters." Instead, the name was up for grabs.

Victims like the fashion retailer H&M faced competing demands for the same stolen data, with no way to know who to pay. And remember the companies that did make a deal? PowerSchool reportedly paid two point eight five million dollars. A few months later, school districts started receiving new ransom demands with samples of the very same data.

They arrested the leader. They detained the" king." And the name lives on. Because ShinyHunters isn't people anymore. It's a brand.

And anyone can wear it.

14:26Protect yourself

So what do you do if a group like this comes for you, or your family? Here's what the FBI itself advises. One. Don't pay, and don't answer their demands.

Paying doesn't buy silence. It proves you'll pay. Two. Verify any urgent call or message through another channel you already trust.

If" your school,"" your bank" or" the police" contacts you, hang up and call the number you know. Three. Save everything: usernames, emails, phone numbers, screenshots. Then report it.

In the U.S., at ic3.gov. In Canada, to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre and your local police. Four. If someone threatens swatting, tell your local police ahead of time, so they know a fake emergency call might come.

Five. Check whether your email has shown up in a breach, change reused passwords, turn on two-step login, and consider freezing your credit. Six. At work, treat any call from" IT support" asking you to approve a connection, install something, or read out a code as a red flag.

Hang up, and call your IT department on the number you already have. That one phone call is how this group got into Google, Qantas and the home security company ADT. Seven. If your school or college was in one of these breaches, expect phishing that uses the real details.

The FBI warns that stolen school data can be used to impersonate teachers, IT staff or the financial aid office. If an email knows your student ID, that doesn't make it real. My personal advice. Tonight at dinner, agree on a code word with your family.

If someone tries to blackmail you, or tells you your child is in danger, ask for the code word. If they don't know it, it's not your loved one. It's not your child. PowerSchool paid, and a few months later, the threats came again.

So this can go on forever. The FBI ended its message to ShinyHunters with one line. I suggest you reach out first while the choice is still yours. For the rest of us, the lesson is simpler.

The hackers are counting on panic. Don't give it to them. Next time: the text messages that empty bank accounts, and how to spot one in two seconds.

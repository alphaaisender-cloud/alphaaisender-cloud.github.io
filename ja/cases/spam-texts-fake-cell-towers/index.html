事件

なぜ急に迷惑SMSがこんなに届くのか？答えの一部はこうです：未払いの通行料、荷物、「不正利用の警告」についてのSMSは、銀行からでも、携帯会社からでもなかったかもしれません。6か国の警察が、車の中から同じ装置を押収しました。SMSブラスター——トランクに積まれた偽の携帯基地局で、何千台もの電話に一斉にメッセージを送ります。東京、ロンドン、トロント、アテネ、ジュネーブ、マレーシアの実際の事件を追い、iPhoneとAndroidで迷惑SMSを止める方法、そして電話に偽基地局を無視させるある設定を紹介します。

迷惑SMSを止める方法（IPHONE、ANDROID、SAMSUNG）

  1. 有料道路会社も銀行も配送業者も、SMSのリンクから支払わせることはありません。アプリや公式サイトを自分で開いてください。
  2. 知らないSMSには返信しないでください。番号をブロックしましょう（Samsung：SMSを開く → ⋮ → 番号をブロック；iPhone：送信者をタップ → この発信者を着信拒否 → 迷惑メッセージを報告）。
  3. 詐欺SMSは7726に転送し（米国・カナダ・英国）、その後削除してください。
  4. ReportFraud.ftc.gov または ic3.gov で通報してください。
  5. 可能なら2Gをオフにしてください（Android：設定 → 接続 → モバイルネットワーク → 2Gを許可；iPhone：ロックダウンモード — 一部のアプリも制限されます）。
  6. 本物の銀行が「安全のため」にお金を移すよう求めたり、送ったばかりのコードを聞くために電話してきたりすることは決してありません。

よくある質問

最近、迷惑SMSが多いのはなぜ？

米国で届け出られたSMS詐欺の被害額は1億3,100万ドル（2021年）から4億7,000万ドル（2024年、FTC）に増えました。「フィッシング・アズ・ア・サービス」のキットが、携帯会社のフィルターが見えないiMessageやRCSを使って何百万通も送信しています。さらに一部は、車で運ばれる偽の携帯基地局、SMSブラスターから送られています。

SMSブラスターとは？

持ち運べる偽の携帯基地局です。近くの電話を2Gに切り替えさせ（2Gでは基地局が本物か確認しない）、任意の送信者名でSMSを送ります。英国、カナダ、日本、ギリシャ、スイス、マレーシアの警察が押収しています。

近くの偽基地局を見つけられる？

たいていは無理です。対策は、2Gをオフにすることと、SMSのリンクからは決して支払わないことです。

通行料のSMSは本物？

ほぼ偽物です。FBIは「未払い通行料12.51ドル」というSMSに注意を呼びかけました。

「すみません、番号を間違えました」？

返信しないでください——STOPとも送らないで。FBIによると、偽の暗号資産投資へ誘い込む手口です。

0:00A fake cell tower in a car

Why are you getting so many spam texts lately? Here's part of the answer. Right now, a car could be parked outside your home, or the mall you're in. In its trunk, a box that pretends to be a cell tower.

Your phone connects to it without asking you. And then you get a text built to take your money. Americans reported losing four hundred seventy million dollars to scam texts in a single year. And that's only what people reported.

Police have now pulled these machines out of cars in six countries. You'll see the real evidence: police video, court records, FBI warnings. Everyone tells you, "don't click the link." We'll show you the one setting that makes a fake tower useless. November seventh, twenty twenty-six.

Saturday. Ginza, the most expensive shopping street in Tokyo. A car is parked at the curb. Around it, thousands of phones suddenly lose their signal.

And then, some of them get a text. Your card payment has been suspended. Tap the link to fix it. Tokyo police say the texts didn't come from a bank, and didn't even come through a phone company.

They came from inside that car. Officers arrested a thirty-one-year-old man on the spot, on suspicion of running an illegal cell tower out of the vehicle. According to Japanese media, in one or two hours, the device knocked more than ten thousand phones off their real network. He denies it.

It's the first case like this in Japan. But not in the world. In the last eighteen months, police have pulled the same kind of machine out of cars in London, Geneva, Athens, Malaysia, and Toronto. When I started digging into this story, I thought it was something simple, something ordinary: someone, somewhere, sitting at a computer and just sending out spam.

It turned out to be even simpler, but much scarier. They literally got into a car and drove to places packed with people. Every phone was their target. Your phone too.

1:48Why so many spam texts lately (toll, package, bank, job, wrong number)

First, the scale. In twenty twenty-four, Americans told the Federal Trade Commission they lost four hundred seventy million dollars to scams that started with a text message. That's more than five times the number from twenty twenty. In twenty twenty-one, it was one hundred thirty-one million.

A year later, three hundred twenty-six million. And the FTC says most people never report it at all. The FTC's top five are probably sitting in your phone right now. A package that couldn't be delivered.

A job offer for rating products online. A fraud alert from your bank asking you to reply yes or NO. An unpaid toll. And a friendly" sorry, wrong number." Two of them cost the most.

The fake fraud alert asks you to reply yes or NO to verify a big purchase. Reply, and you're connected to a fake fraud department that pushes you to move your money somewhere" safe." The wrong-number text is slower. It starts as a friendly chat, becomes a friendship, sometimes a romance, and ends on a fake investment platform. The FTC says people often lose tens of thousands of dollars that way.

Your real bank will never ask you to move money to keep it safe. In April twenty twenty-four, the FBI put out a public warning about one of them. More than two thousand complaints in about six weeks, all about the same text. We've noticed an outstanding toll amount of twelve dollars and fifty-one cents.

To avoid a late fee of fifty dollars, visit this link." The link goes to a site that looks like your toll company. The twelve dollars is bait. What they want is your card number. Here's the first rule, straight from the FBI.

If a text says you owe money, don't touch the link. Open your toll company's website or app yourself, the way you always do, and check your account there. If you really owe something, it will be there. And if that rule just saved you twelve dollars and fifty-one cents, or a lot more, hit like.

It helps this video reach someone who needs it. So who writes these texts? Most of them start in the same place. Security researchers call it the Smishing Triad, a network they trace to China, which sells phishing as a service.

For a subscription, any criminal can get ready-made fake websites for the Postal Service, toll companies and banks, and a control panel to run the whole operation. And the writing is getting automated. In June twenty twenty-five, a consumer advocate testified before Congress that his team had asked ChatGPT for urgent texts begging for fifty dollars in bitcoin. It produced more than a hundred, complete with common names and real hospitals.

Every message slightly different. And that, he warned, is exactly what helps scam texts slip past spam filters. So we've kind of moved away from warning people about grammatical errors and spelling errors and we're really kind of just sharing with people to watch for that sense of urgency and to take a beat. Talk And here's the first reason your phone company doesn't STOP them.

Many of these messages don't travel as regular texts at all. Researchers found they're often sent through iMessage and RCS, the internet-based chat that looks exactly like a text. To your carrier's spam filter, they're practically invisible. Then came a twist.

The first one to strike back wasn't the police. It was Google. In November twenty twenty-five, Google sued the people behind the newest version of this kit, called Lighthouse, in federal court in New York. It used the racketeering law written for the mafia.

The defendants have no names, just" Does one through twenty-five," believed to be in China. Within days, Lighthouse told its own customers that its servers had been shut down. But researchers saw parts of the operation keep running. Because when a factory can sell its templates to anyone, you can't sue every buyer.

And not every scam text is selling you a fake toll. Some of them just say hello.

5:10"Sorry, wrong number" text scam — who is really texting you

There's one text on the FTC's list that doesn't ask for your card number. At least, not yet. Hi, is this Jessica? Are we still on for dinner Friday?" You reply," Sorry, wrong number." And that's exactly the reply they were waiting for.

The FBI's El Paso office describes what happens next. The stranger apologizes, keeps chatting, and slowly becomes a friend, sometimes a romance. It's all a ruse," the FBI writes, designed to make you relax. Weeks later, your new friend mentions a crypto platform that's been making them rich.

You try it. The balance goes up. Until you try to withdraw. So who's on the other end of that wrong number?

In October twenty twenty-five, the Justice Department gave one answer. Prosecutors in Brooklyn charged Chen Zhi, the chairman of a Cambodian conglomerate called Prince Group. They allege his company ran scam compounds behind high walls and barbed wire, where trafficked workers were forced to run these schemes, often under threat of violence. And inside, prosecutors say, were phone farms.

Automated call centers with thousands of phones and millions of mobile numbers. That's the factory behind the friendly stranger who texted the wrong number. Then the government struck back. The Justice Department went after about one hundred twenty-seven thousand bitcoin, worth around fifteen billion dollars, already in U.S. custody.

It called it the largest forfeiture action in its history. Chen Zhi never made it to Brooklyn. In January twenty twenty-six, Cambodia arrested him and sent him, not to America, but to China. His company has denied any wrongdoing.

And on November fifth, twenty twenty-six, the U.S. Treasury sanctioned forty-one more companies and four more people it says keep the Prince Group network running. The FBI's advice here sounds almost rude. Don't respond to unknown texts at all.

Not even with STOP, even if the message tells you to. Any reply shows there's a real person on this number. Just block it and report it. I'll admit it: I used to be the one who'd politely text back," you've got the wrong number." It's a normal human reaction, but that's exactly what the scammers are counting on.

That's their hook. Just don't reply, and block the number.

7:13How a fake cell tower beats your carrier's spam filter

Now back to the cars. Some criminals decided they didn't need the internet, or a phone farm, at all. They bought a cell tower. Police call the device an SMS blaster.

Your phone is always looking for the strongest cell tower nearby. An SMS blaster simply shouts louder than the real one. And your phone switches to it. There's a trick inside.

The blaster pushes your phone down to the old 2G network, a standard from the nineteen nineties. On 2G, your phone has to prove who it is to the tower, but the tower never has to prove anything to your phone. So your phone can't tell a real tower from a box in a trunk. And you'd never notice.

Your signal flickers for a moment, the text arrives, and your phone goes back to the real network as if nothing happened. The message just sits in your inbox, looking like every other text from your bank. Once your phone is connected, the blaster can send it anything, with any sender name it likes. Your bank.

The tax office. The post office. In the words of London police, criminals can bypass the safeguards designed to block malicious sender names and harmful links. The message never touches your carrier's network, so its filter never sees it.

And here's the part that answers another question people keep asking: how did they get my number? With a blaster, they don't need it. They don't text you. They text everyone around them.

You were just standing in the wrong place. Here's what shocked me the most. For years we've been teaching each other: don't give your number to strangers, double-check who's calling you, who's texting you. But here, your number isn't needed at all.

Picture this: you're just standing at a bus STOP. A car is parked nearby, and inside it there's a device, at first glance just a box, that automatically sends you messages. You think you're talking to your bank, but these messages never even reach your carrier.

8:57London: the SMS blaster that texted the police

March twenty twenty-five. London. A student from China named Ruichen Xiong has run up serious gambling debts. Someone offers him a way to pay them off.

All he has to do is drive. For six days, he drives a black Honda around London with an SMS blaster in the trunk, following routes and timings he's been given. Tens of thousands of phones in range. And then he makes a mistake no script could plan for.

He drives right past police officers. Their phones buzz. A text from HMRC, Britain's tax office. Except the officers know it isn't.

The blaster had just texted the police themselves. They STOP the car with the device still running in the back. That summer, Xiong is sentenced to more than a year in prison. But a driver isn't a boss.

Britain's specialist card fraud unit kept pulling the thread. Across its blaster investigations, it arrested seven people and seized seven of these devices. And in this case, the thread led to a man named Di Li. Police say Li, forty-three, from Finchley, arranged the device, helped get the car, and paid Xiong 's living costs.

The routes were coordinated remotely, by Li and others in a wider network. Police describe those drives as a" testing" phase. A test run, in the middle of London. Officers searched his home in August twenty twenty-five and found his messages with Xiong about the blaster.

He was arrested on September first. In court, Li had an explanation. The machine, he said, was for advertising. He was just a middleman for someone in China, trying to help a young man pay off his debts.

The jury didn't buy it. In June twenty twenty-six, he was sentenced to forty-eight months. And Xiong wasn't even the first. Back in May twenty twenty-four, police found a man sitting in a van in the car park of the Trafford Centre, a huge shopping mall near Manchester.

Hidden in wooden compartments in the back was an active SMS blaster, with aerials on the roof. Authorities called it the first one they had ever found in Britain. His texts pretended to be from Royal Mail. And on his phones, police found seven thousand, eight hundred and fifty-nine stolen card numbers.

In August twenty twenty-six, Mohammed Faiyaz Iqbal, forty-three, was sentenced to three years and eight months. The detective on the case gave one piece of advice, and it works in the U.S., Canada and the U.K. too. If you get a suspicious text, don't reply. Forward it to 7726.

On your keypad, that spells SPAM. Your carrier uses those reports to block the next wave.

11:13Toronto: Project Lighthouse — 13 million disconnections, 911 at risk

November twenty twenty-five. Toronto. A security partner warns police about something it's never seen in Canada: an SMS blaster, working downtown. Police open an investigation.

They call it Project Lighthouse. This blaster didn't stay in one place either. It moved around the Greater Toronto Area in vehicles. Police believe tens of thousands of devices connected to it over several months.

And then the investigators found the number that changes this story. We also identified more than 13 million network disruptions where devices were unable to properly connect to legitimate cell towers. That's significant because during those moments, access to services like 911 could be impacted. Thirteen million moments when phones couldn't properly connect to a real tower.

Picture needing an ambulance while a stranger's car is parked outside, hijacking your signal to sell you a fake parking fine. On March thirty-first, twenty twenty-six, police searched homes in Markham and Hamilton. Two men were arrested, and officers seized several SMS blasters. Three weeks later, a third man walked into a police station and turned himself in.

Together, the three face forty-four charges, including fraud and mischief endangering life. They are presumed innocent. The image you see here is an example of an SMS blaster from an investigation in the UK. The ones we seized in Toronto were uniquely built, and we're not sharing those publicly for safety reasons.

Toronto's deputy police chief put it simply. This wasn't targeting a single individual or a business. It had the ability to reach thousands of devices at once. Police say they got there with help from the RCMP's national cybercrime centre, neighboring police forces, banks and phone companies.

And the lead investigator gave advice that works anywhere. Only access your banking through official apps or by typing the website directly into your browser. And Here's my simple advice. I've done it on my own phone.

This trap works through the old 2G network. You can simply switch it off. On an iPhone, Lockdown Mode does it. On newer Android phones, there's a separate switch called Allow 2G.

Five seconds, and your phone STOPs trusting fake towers. Let me show you where and how to set it up. One honest warning. Lockdown Mode is Apple's strictest security setting, and it also limits some apps and websites.

So it's a trade-off, not a free switch. On Android, the 2G option only appears if your phone and carrier support it. And on the newest Android version, some phones can even warn you when your connection suddenly drops to an unencrypted network.

13:49Greece and Geneva: shark fins and fake parking fines

January twenty twenty-six. Spata, just outside Athens. Greek police STOP a car for a routine check after reports of suspicious behavior. The men inside allegedly show forged ID documents.

Then officers look up, at the roof. On the roof, there's what looks like an ordinary shark-fin antenna, the kind on millions of cars. It wasn't. It was a transmitter, wired to a computer hidden in the trunk.

Police say it forced nearby phones from 4G down to 2G and sent texts pretending to be banks and courier companies. In Geneva, the bait was fake parking fines. Between July and September twenty twenty-five, police arrested three men, aged twenty-one, twenty-one and thirty. Two of them were caught in vehicles carrying SMS blasters.

The texts led to a fake copy of the city's official parking-fines website. They are presumed innocent. Look at the map. Different countries, different police, different languages.

But the same box, the same trick, the same car. In London and in Tokyo, the trail led back to China. Who builds the boxes, and who ships them around the world, hasn't been publicly proven yet. But the people driving them are getting caught.

14:53Is America next? + how to stop spam texts

Back to Tokyo. On Monday, November ninth, police sent the man from Ginza to prosecutors. Japanese media report eighteen complaints so far, and losses of about four million yen. Investigators are now checking whether the same equipment was used elsewhere in the city.

He still denies everything. And here's the question for us. London, Geneva, Athens, Malaysia, Toronto, Tokyo. As of this video, we found no public arrest for an SMS blaster in the United States.

That doesn't mean they aren't here. It means nobody has caught one in public yet. The texts, as we saw, are already here by the millions. I'll tell you honestly what I think.

If a car like this was caught in Toronto, just an hour's flight from New York, then the question isn't when it shows up on American roads. The question is how fast we can spot it. While police are learning to catch and track down these cars, I suggest you do something yourself, and protect yourself tonight. Here's what I did myself.

Four things, under a minute. One: no toll company, bank, or delivery service will ever make you pay through a link in a text. Open their app or website yourself. Two: forward the scam to 7726, then delete it.

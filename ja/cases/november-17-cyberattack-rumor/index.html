事件

銀行残高ゼロ。90日間のインターネット停止。国民に備えと食料の備蓄を呼びかける33か国の地図。

数百万人がこの「2026年11月17日の緊急警告」を目にしました。

私たちは最初の投稿までさかのぼり、8つの政府が実際に何と言ったかと照らし合わせてファクトチェックしました：米国、カナダ、英国、ドイツ、フランス、ベルギー、ノルウェー、フィンランド。

この調査の内容

  • 11月17日のデマを始めたのは誰か——そして本人さえ未確認だと認めた理由
  • 「33か国で緊急事態」の地図と「2026年 食料を備蓄せよ」の投稿が実際に示すもの（英国は30日ではなく3日と言っている）
  • 日付つきの「サイバー攻撃警告」すべてに共通する5つの材料——次のデマを見抜くために
  • 本当のこと：欧州のスーパーの冷凍庫、米軍の売店、Turning Stoneカジノへのサイバー攻撃、そしてVolt Typhoon
  • 「90日間のネット停止」予言がインターネットの仕組みを誤解している理由——そして銀行がドミノのように倒れない理由
  • FBIと10か国の警察がQakbot、Volt Typhoonの隠しルーター、LockBitを壊滅させた方法
  • 72時間の非常用キット、落ち着いたバージョン。シェルターは不要です。

72時間の非常用キット（FEMA、カナダ、英国が一致して勧めるもの）

  1. 水：1人1日約4リットル、3日分
  2. 普段食べていて調理不要の食料、3日分
  3. 少額紙幣の現金（カード端末とATMが真っ先に止まります）
  4. 1週間分の薬、充電済みのモバイルバッテリー、懐中電灯、電池式ラジオ
  5. 大切な電話番号を紙に：家族、銀行、医師
  6. 本物の警報だけを：ready.gov（米国）、Alert Ready と getprepared.gc.ca（カナダ）、gov.uk/prepare（英国）。アドレスは自分で入力してください。
  7. うわさでお金を動かさないでください。「銀行」や「警察」から今すぐ行動しろという電話？切って、カード裏面の番号に電話してください。
  8. 家族の計画：合言葉、集合場所、一人暮らしの人を誰が見に行くか。

全文

ドキュメンタリーのナレーション全文をチャプターごとに掲載。タイムスタンプを押すと、その場面から動画が開きます。 文字起こしは作品の言語である英語です。

0:00What is happening on November 17?

November seventeenth. Hackers break into the world's biggest banks. Every balance drops to zero. Then the internet goes dark.

For three months. One version of this warning has been viewed more than five million times. A map of thirty-three countries telling their citizens to stockpile food and water is everywhere. A former deputy director of the FBI told Americans to keep thirty days of food and water at home.

In early November, hackers really did get into supermarkets across Europe, and turned off the freezers. And in New York, three casinos suddenly couldn't take a single credit card. So, what is happening on November seventeenth? We traced this warning back to the very first post, and checked it against what eight governments actually told their citizens.

Here's who started it. Who profits from your fear. And what hackers can really do to you, today.

0:54Who started the November 17 hoax

The first post we could find was written in Portuguese, by an account on X calling itself Wesley. His source? Someone on the dark web. No name.

No proof. No technical details. And in the same post, he admitted the information was not confirmed. On November second, Russian-language Telegram channels picked it up.

Then Threads. Then TikTok. With every share, the story grew. Stock up on food.

On water. On cash. Buy gold and silver. A super-powerful artificial intelligence would fix the internet while it was down.

And then, quote," COVID two point oh." A second claim had started a few days earlier, on October twenty-seventh. Seventeen countries and the European Union, it said, had told their citizens to stock up. That post alone drew about five point three million views. Within a week, the two stories merged into one.

Fact-checkers in Kazakhstan were among the first to look. Their verdict: the government notices were ordinary preparedness leaflets, and the list of countries was stitched together from reports that had nothing to do with each other.

1:57What 8 governments actually said

So we went to the source. Not the screenshots. The government websites themselves. Eight countries.

Here's what they actually said. Britain. On October twenty-second, the Cabinet Office launched a campaign called Be Ready. Its own words:" small, simple steps," in case everyday services are temporarily disrupted.

Three days of water and food. Three liters of drinking water per person, per day. Some cash. A radio.

A power bank. It's a three-year campaign. Not a countdown. Germany.

Its civil protection office published a new guide in October twenty twenty-five. The ideal: ten days of supplies. But it says three days already help a lot. Here's the detail nobody shared: the old guide said fourteen days.

Germany didn't raise the bar. It lowered it. France. Its guide is called" Tous responsables," everyone responsible.

A seventy-two-hour kit. The government is clear that it's a recommendation, not an obligation. And there is no" official government kit" for sale. If someone tries to sell you one, that's a red flag.

Belgium. Its National Crisis Center asks people to manage on their own for at least three days. Twenty-eight pages, and the word" war" doesn't appear once. Belgium's interior minister summed it up in four words: never panic, never be naive.

Norway asks for one week, and mailed its booklet to all two point two million households in the country. One of its tips is surprisingly practical: have accounts in more than one bank, in case one has a technical problem. Finland's guide goes one step further. Alongside water and food, it teaches people to recognize information influence.

In other words, how to spot exactly the kind of story you're watching us take apart right now. Canada says be ready to take care of yourself for at least seventy-two hours, and keep some cash in small bills. And in the United States, FEMA's advice hasn't changed in years: about a gallon of water per person, per day, for several days. And November seventeenth?

It does appear on one government website. FEMA's. At about two twenty in the afternoon Eastern time, FEMA and the FCC will run a national test of the Emergency Alert System, on TV and radio. It was announced in September.

The law requires one at least every three years. FEMA's exact words: no action is required. Eight governments. Not one of them mentions November seventeenth.

What they describe is a seatbelt. Not a countdown. So how did a seatbelt become a doomsday? Newsweek put these programs on one map, thirty-three countries, and on social media the map became proof the whole world was preparing for the same day.

Dan Bongino, the former FBI deputy director, did tell his podcast audience to keep thirty days of food and water. He pointed to, quote," warnings put out by the U.K. government and elsewhere about thirty days of preparedness." But the British checklist says three days. Not thirty. Somewhere between London and a podcast, three became thirty.

Someone even added a screenshot from a twenty twenty-three Simpsons episode about a blackout, as a" prediction." The episode names no date.

5:00The recipe for a scare (and who profits)

Every scare like this is built from the same five ingredients. Learn them once, and you'll recognize the next one. Because there will be a next one. A new date.

A new" insider." One. An anonymous insider. A source on the dark web." Nobody can check it, and nobody can disprove it. Two.

An exact date. A date makes it feel planned, and starts a countdown in your head. Three. Real facts as glue.

A real campaign, a real test, a real map. Four. A name you trust. A former FBI official, a famous cartoon, a news channel.

Five. A push to act. Stock up now. Share before it's deleted.

Buy gold. Move your money. It isn't new. A fake virus warning called" Dance of the Hillary" has been forwarded around the world since at least twenty fifteen.

When Spain and Portugal lost power in April twenty twenty-five, fake reports blamed a cyberattack within hours. Investigators found no cyberattack. And in the very same week as this rumor, hoax bomb threats hit flights across Europe. On a single day, at least twenty-five of them.

Planes landed and were searched. No explosives were found. So who profits? First, attention.

One video calling November" not a drill" pulled more than two hundred thousand views in six days. Fear is the best-performing content on the internet. Second, sales. Survival kits.

Gold. Crypto, sold as an escape from banks that are about to be wiped. And kits sold as" official," when no official kit exists. And third, the ones who profit most: scammers.

Fear is their best opening line. Your bank is about to be hacked. Move your savings to a safe account." The Federal Trade Commission is blunt: nobody who works there will ever tell you to move your money to protect it. And between January twenty twenty-five and July twenty twenty-six, the FBI received nearly sixty-one thousand complaints about criminals impersonating police and government officials.

More than one point six billion dollars lost. Fear can do more damage than the hackers themselves. In May twenty twenty-one, a ransomware attack shut down Colonial Pipeline, which carries fuel to the U.S. East Coast.

The pipeline was down for days. But the empty gas stations? At the peak, around seventy percent of stations in North Carolina had no fuel. North Carolina's governor said the shortages were pretty much related to panic buying.

The hackers stopped a pipeline. The panic emptied the pumps.

7:23Cyber attack in Europe: what's real

Now, the part that's real. In October, attackers took remote control of the refrigeration systems in hundreds of supermarkets across France, and sent the temperatures up. On November second, it happened again. Supermarkets in Sweden, Belgium, Luxembourg, Ireland and Estonia.

Fridges and freezers failing overnight. Food thrown away. The same day, several French blood banks were hit by a cyberattack. The health ministry said no blood products were compromised.

The European Commission called emergency meetings with its cyber agency ENISA and with Europol. Its spokesperson said these were not isolated incidents. The EU's agriculture commissioner called them a deliberate attempt to disrupt Europe's food supply chain. Carrefour Belgium said fifteen of its stores were hit.

In Ireland, thirteen SuperValu stores. In Estonia, attackers got into the computer controlling one store's system, and set the temperature to fifty degrees Celsius. Two pro-Russian hacker groups, NoName zero five seven sixteen and Z-Pentest, claimed responsibility on Telegram. Investigators haven't confirmed it, and some researchers think the groups are simply taking credit for the headlines.

The Kremlin called the claims absurd. The weak spot is real either way. Researchers at Forescout found nearly thirteen thousand refrigeration control systems exposed online, in ninety-nine countries, many running old software. Z-Pentest isn't new.

In December twenty twenty-four, a Danish water utility was hit, and homes were left without water. Denmark's intelligence service blamed the group. In July twenty twenty-six, the EU and Britain sanctioned it together, for the first time. And in America?

In late August, freezers failed at commissaries, the grocery stores on U.S. military bases. At least six bases. At one of them, every freezer in the store switched into defrost mode overnight. The Pentagon is now investigating it as a suspected cyberattack, together with French investigators.

It hasn't confirmed that anyone broke in. And on November second, in upstate New York, Turning Stone, the casinos run by the Oneida Indian Nation, reported a cybersecurity incident. Three casinos went cash only. No credit cards.

No ATMs. The company says it found no evidence that personal data was accessed. But while it lasted, if you didn't have cash, you couldn't pay. In the United States, the most serious threat is quieter.

In twenty twenty-four, the FBI, the NSA and the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency revealed that a Chinese state group called Volt Typhoon had been hiding inside American critical infrastructure. Communications. Energy. Transportation.

Water. In some networks, for at least five years. The FBI's director told Congress that China's hackers were positioning on American infrastructure, quote," in preparation to wreak havoc and cause real-world harm," if or when China decides the time has come to strike. And it has happened before.

In December twenty fifteen, hackers cut power to about two hundred twenty-five thousand people in Ukraine, for several hours. In twenty seventeen, a malicious program called NotPetya spread from Ukraine to companies around the world, from the shipping giant Maersk to the drugmaker Merck. The White House called it the most destructive and costly cyberattack in history. Billions of dollars in damage.

So yes. Hackers can turn off freezers. Cut the water to people's homes. Stop a pipeline.

Knock the card machines out. Black out a region for a few hours. That's what is really in their hands. Now look at what isn't.

10:54Why the internet and banks don't fall

The internet isn't one machine. It's tens of thousands of independent networks, run by different companies in different countries. There is no switch that turns it off. When the internet really does vanish for months, it's because a government shut it down inside its own borders.

Not because someone on Telegram pressed a button. Banks don't keep your money as one number on one computer. They keep copies, in different places. In the United States, deposits are insured by the FDIC, up to two hundred fifty thousand dollars per depositor, per bank.

In Canada, the CDIC covers up to one hundred thousand. Britain and the EU have their own guarantees. And every attack makes the next one harder. After France, supermarket chains across Europe moved their fridges onto secured connections.

After NotPetya, companies rebuilt their networks. When one country finds a hole, the warning goes out to everyone, in the open. That's why one hit doesn't knock down the whole board. Remember July twenty twenty-four?

One faulty software update crashed eight and a half million Windows computers. Airports, hospitals, banks. It wasn't even a hack. Most systems were back within days.

The real danger came afterwards: within hours, scammers were sending fake" fix" messages to frightened people. And when hackers cross the line, governments hit back. In August twenty twenty-three, the FBI and its partners took over Qakbot, a network of more than seven hundred thousand infected computers, and switched it off. In January twenty twenty-four, the FBI went into hundreds of hijacked routers that Volt Typhoon was hiding behind, and cut them off.

A month later, police from ten countries seized LockBit, the most harmful ransomware gang in the world, on its own servers. Hackers are fast. But they are not stronger than every government on Earth, working together. No government wants chaos in its own country.

That's exactly why they publish these guides in the open, in plain words, for every household. Germany lowered its number. Belgium says never panic. Finland teaches people to spot manipulation.

A three-day kit isn't a sign the end is coming. It's a seatbelt.

12:58Your 72 hour emergency kit

So here's what to actually do. No bunker. No boat. No hundred pounds of canned food.

Just the things the official guides agree on, for the real risk: a storm, a power cut, a hacked system, for a day or three. One. Water. About a gallon per person, per day.

Four liters. For three days. Bottled is fine. Two.

Food you already eat, that doesn't need cooking. Three days' worth. Three. Some cash, in small bills.

You saw it in New York: when systems go down, card readers and ATMs go first. Four. A week of your medicines. A charged power bank.

A flashlight. A battery radio, if you have one. Five. Write down the phone numbers that matter.

Family. Your bank. Your doctor. On paper.

Your contacts live in the cloud, and your phone battery won't last forever. Six. Know where the real warnings come from. In the U.S., emergency alerts and ready dot gov.

In Canada, Alert Ready and getprepared dot ca. In the U.K., gov dot uk slash prepare. Type the address yourself. Never trust a forwarded link that claims to be the government.

Seven. Protect your money from the panic, not from the date. Never move your money because of a rumor. Turn on alerts in your bank's app.

If someone calls from your" bank" or the" police" and tells you to act now, hang up, and call the number on the back of your card. And like Norway says: if you can, don't keep every dollar in a single bank. Eight. Make a family plan.

A code word, so nobody can fake a call from you. A place to meet. And who checks on the people who live alone. And before you share the next warning, ask three questions.

Who is the source? Is there an official government page that says the same thing? And what does it want you to do? If the answer is panic, pay, or forward, you already know.

14:45November 18

On November eighteenth, you'll wake up. Your money will still be in your account. The internet will still be on. And somewhere, someone will already be writing the next warning, with a new date.

Now you know the recipe. An anonymous source. A date. Real facts as glue.

A trusted name. A push to act. The real threats are smaller, and local, and the people who defend us share what they learn. What hurts people most isn't the hack.

It's the panic, and the stranger who calls when you're afraid. Water. A little cash. A list of numbers.

A plan with the people you love. That's it. No bunker. Never panic.

Never be naive. Next time, a group of hackers that did something very real. They broke into the FBI. And the FBI came for them.
